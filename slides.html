Part A

Measuring information

1Probability and logs2What is information?3Surprise and the bit4Entropy5Shared information
Five lessons that build one measuring stick for information: the bit.

Logs count yes/no answers

  • A probability is a number from 0 (never) to 1 (always); all outcomes add up to 1; independent events multiply
  • 2ᵇ = n ⇔ log₂(n) = b: b bits can write n codes, and n things need log₂(n) bits
  • log₂ counts halvings: 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1 is 4 steps, so log₂(16) = 4
  • For probabilities (below 1), log₂ is negative; logs turn × into +
log₂(n) is the number of fair yes/no answers needed to pick one of n things.
Light switches: one switch has 2 positions, three give 2³ = 8 patterns; for 8 rooms you need log₂(8) = 3 switches. A bit (binary digit) is exactly such a switch. Probability is slices of one pizza: they always make one whole. The expected value is a weighted average: a fair die averages (1 + … + 6) × 1/6 = 3.5. Entropy (two slides on) is exactly this kind of average.

Information is what reduces uncertainty

  • Claude Shannon, 1948: information is what you didn't know before, not what a message means
  • Every communication: source → encoder → channel (noise) → decoder → receiver
  • Shannon's two big questions: how small can a message be (compression)? How fast can it travel through noise (capacity)?
"The sun rose" tells you almost nothing; "it snowed in the desert" tells you a lot. Information measures surprise, not meaning.
Leaving meaning out on purpose is what made the theory work for text, sound, pictures and DNA alike. "Bit" has two meanings: a binary digit (a 0 or 1 on a wire) and a unit of information; one binary digit carries at most one bit of information, and less when it is easy to predict. Phones, Wi-Fi, USB, streaming and space probes all follow this chain.

Rare events carry more bits

The surprise (self-information) of an event with probability p is −log₂(p) bits.

EventpSurprise
The sun rises≈ 1≈ 0 bits
A coin lands heads1/21 bit
A die shows 61/62.58 bits
Ace of spades1/525.70 bits
Win a 1-in-a-million draw1/1,000,00019.93 bits
Halve the probability, add one bit. One fair yes/no answer is exactly 1 bit.
News value: "dog bites man" is no story, "man bites dog" is. Surprises of independent events add up: heads and a 6 has p = 1/12 and surprise log₂(12) ≈ 3.58 = 1 + 2.58, which is why Shannon used a logarithm. A random 4-digit PIN carries about 13.3 bits. This same curve becomes the AI training loss (the classifier slide in Part B).

Entropy is the average surprise

  • H = −Σ p(x) log₂ p(x): each outcome's probability × its surprise, added up
  • A 90/10 coin: H = 0.9 × 0.152 + 0.1 × 3.32 ≈ 0.47 bits
  • 0 when the outcome is certain; largest when all outcomes are equally likely
  • Maximum = log₂(number of outcomes): a fair coin 1 bit, four equal weathers 2 bits
Entropy is highest when you have no idea what comes next.
Desert weather is predictable (low entropy); weather that changes every day is not (high entropy). English: 26 equal letters would carry log₂(26) ≈ 4.7 bits each; letter frequencies give about 4.1; with context, Shannon's 1951 guessing experiments estimated about 1 bit per letter. That gap is why text compresses so well. Security people talk about "bits of entropy" in passwords and keys.

Mutual information: what two things share

  • I(X;Y) = the bits of uncertainty about X that disappear once you know Y
  • I(X;Y) = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y)
Umbrella matches the weatherI(X;Y)H(X|Y) left
50% (random)01 bit
80% (usually)0.28 bits0.72 bits
100% (always)1 bit0
0 when the two are unrelated; as large as the entropy itself when one fully decides the other.
If you can't see the sky but you see your neighbour's umbrella, you can guess the weather better. Mutual information is symmetric, and it catches any kind of relationship, not only straight-line ones like correlation. It reappears as information gain in decision trees and as channel capacity (both in Part B).
Part B

Using information

6Compression7Noisy channels8Cross-entropy and KL9Information theory in AI
The bit at work: compression, error correction, the cost of a wrong belief, and how AI models are trained and scored.

Compression gives short codes to common symbols

  • Text ABACABAD…: A ½, B ¼, C ⅛, D ⅛ → codes A = 0, B = 10, C = 110, D = 111
  • Average: ½ × 1 + ¼ × 2 + ⅛ × 3 + ⅛ × 3 = 1.75 bits per letter, instead of 8 (plain text) or 2 (fixed length)
  • No code is the start of another, so 010110 can only be A B C
A good code spends about −log₂(p) bits on a symbol, so its average length is the entropy: the limit no lossless code can beat.
Morse code already did this in the 1800s: E is one dot, Q is dash-dash-dot-dash. The demo builds its codes with Huffman coding; only the result matters: common letters get short codes. Code lengths 1, 2, 3, 3 are exactly the surprises of the four letters.

You can't compress randomness, unless you drop details

Equally likely symbols leave nothing to shorten: 16 equal letters need log₂(16) = 4 bits each; zipping a zip file saves almost nothing.

LosslessLossy
Keepsevery bit, the exact originalonly what people notice
Limitthe entropyfar below, by dropping information
ExamplesZIP, PNG, FLACJPEG, MP3, streaming video
Lossless compression stops at the entropy; lossy compression goes lower by throwing details away on purpose.
Real tools such as ZIP also replace repeated pieces ("THE ") with short references; their limit is the entropy of the text with all its patterns, about 1 bit per English letter. In AI, a model that predicts the next word well also compresses text well: prediction and compression are the same skill.

Smart redundancy fights noise

ProtectionExtra costDetects?Fixes?
None0nono
Parity bit (1 per 4 bits)+25%yes, one flipno: asks for a resend

With 10% noise, Repeat ×3 is wrong only when 2 or 3 copies flip: 2.8% instead of 10%.

Useful redundancy (planned check bits) lets the receiver notice, or even fix, flipped bits.
On a bad phone line you spell "A as in Apple, B as in Banana": the extra words are redundancy. Parity: for 1011 (three 1s) the extra bit is 1, so 10111 is sent; an odd count at the receiver means a bit flipped. 3 × 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.9 + 0.1³ = 0.027 + 0.001 = 0.028.

Every channel has a speed limit: its capacity

  • Capacity = the most information per bit sent that can get through: the largest mutual information between input and output
  • Below capacity, smart codes make errors as rare as you like; above it, no code works
  • A channel that flips 10% of bits: capacity = 1 − H(0.1) = 1 − 0.47 = 0.53 bits per bit sent
  • Repeat ×3 carries only 0.33 bits per bit sent, and still has 2.8% errors
Shannon proved the limit exists in 1948; it took engineers about 45 years to build codes that come close.
Compression removes useless redundancy; error correction adds useful redundancy. Real systems do both: compress first, then protect. Codes in use: Hamming codes in ECC memory; Reed–Solomon in QR codes, CDs and deep-space probes; LDPC and turbo codes in Wi-Fi, 4G and 5G, close to Shannon's limit.

A wrong belief costs extra bits

  • Cross-entropy H(p, q) = −Σ p(x) log₂ q(x): your average surprise when the world follows p but you believe q
  • KL(p‖q) = H(p, q) − H(p): the extra cost, always 0 or more, and 0 only when q = p
Belief about the weather (true H = 1.75)Cross-entropyKL
Correct (½, ¼, ⅛, ⅛)1.75 bits0
Uniform (¼ each)2.00 bits0.25 bits
Very wrong (0.05, 0.05, 0.45, 0.45)≈ 3.53 bits≈ 1.78 bits
Cross-entropy = the unavoidable entropy + the KL penalty for believing the wrong thing.
A traveller who packs for sun when it often rains is surprised, at a cost, every rainy day. In compression terms, a code built from belief q costs −log₂(q) bits per symbol, so its average is the cross-entropy and the waste is KL. Never be 100% sure: if q gives an outcome 0 and it happens, the surprise is infinite, which is why AI models never give a real outcome a probability of exactly 0.

Direction matters: KL is not a distance

  • Two distributions over red, green and blue balls: p = (0.10, 0.40, 0.50), q = (0.80, 0.15, 0.05)
  • H(p, q) = 3.288 bits, but H(q, p) = 2.906 bits
  • KL(p‖q) ≈ 1.927 bits, but KL(q‖p) ≈ 2.022 bits
The weights come from the first distribution, so each direction cares most about the events it thinks are common.
A true distance is the same both ways and 0 for identical distributions; cross-entropy is neither (H(p, p) is the entropy of p, about 1.36 bits here). KL fixes the second property (KL(p‖p) = 0) but not the first. KL is used to compare a model before and after fine-tuning.

Training a classifier makes the right answer less surprising

  • When the truth is "cat", cross-entropy has one term: loss = −log₂ q(cat), the surprise of the right answer
  • q = 0.9 → 0.15 bits · q = 0.5 → 1 bit · q = 0.01 → 6.64 bits
  • ML libraries use the natural log, so the loss is in nats: 1 nat ≈ 1.44 bits
The loss is the surprise curve from Part A: confident mistakes are punished hardest.
An AI model is a guesser: it gives probabilities, never certainty. Information theory gives the scoreboard: a good guesser is rarely surprised by the truth. ML calls this the negative log-likelihood (NLL); averaged over a dataset it is the cross-entropy loss (F.cross_entropy, NLLLoss in PyTorch).

Decision trees ask the most informative question

  • Information gain = entropy before − average entropy after = the mutual information between the question and the answer
  • 16 days, 8 played outside: H = 1 bit
  • "Is it the weekend?" → 4 days (all played, H = 0) and 12 days (4 played, H ≈ 0.918) → gain ≈ 0.31 bits
  • "Is it windy?" splits 4/4 and 4/4 → gain 0
The best question is the one that removes the most uncertainty.
Like the fair yes/no questions of the surprise slide: a good question splits the possibilities; a bad one ("is it 7?") usually gets "no" and teaches little. Average after = 4/16 × 0 + 12/16 × 0.918 ≈ 0.69.

Perplexity: how many words a language model is choosing between

  • Score a language model on real text: average the surprise of each actual next word
  • Perplexity = 2^(average surprise)
  • An easy sentence: average 1.38 bits → perplexity ≈ 2.6; an odd sentence: 5.81 bits → ≈ 56
Lower perplexity = a better model of the language: it is less surprised by real text.
Perplexity 2.6 means the model was, on average, as unsure as if it were choosing between about 2.6 equally likely words. LLM APIs report "logprobs" (natural log); ML libraries write perplexity = exp(average NLL), the same number. The probabilities in the demo are invented, but real models are scored exactly this way.

This is the big picture of the course: one number, −log₂ p, links all three. 8 × log₂(26) ≈ 37.6; log₂(4) = 2.

Key takeaways

  1. log₂(n) counts the yes/no answers needed to pick one of n things.
  2. Information is what reduces uncertainty, not what a message means.
  3. Surprise = −log₂(p): rare events carry more bits.
  4. Entropy is the average surprise, highest when everything is equally likely.
  5. Mutual information is what two things share.
  6. Entropy is the limit of lossless compression.
  7. Redundancy fights noise; capacity is the speed limit.
  8. Cross-entropy = entropy + the KL cost of a wrong belief.
  9. AI models are trained and scored by surprise: loss, information gain, perplexity.
  10. Predicting, compressing and communicating are one problem.
